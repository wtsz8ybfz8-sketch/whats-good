Six seconds to a decision.

The whole product is one job: turn "I'm hungry and I can't decide" into a place, before the person gives up and opens Instagram. Everything below is measured against that.

The spine

00:00Open

Location and local hour read on launch. No city picker, no permission wall before value.

00:02Recognise

Six occasions that are plausible at this hour. The person sees their own situation on screen.

00:04Pick

One tap. Results appear immediately — no Search button standing between intent and answer.

00:10Narrow

Only if needed. Distance, spend, party as drags; neighbourhood as chips.

00:20Commit

Directions, call, book, or save. The exit has to be as fast as the entry.

Tap budget

5+Before

Type, or pick a mood, then Search, then filter, then scroll. Five moves and a decision still not made.

1Now — the common case

Open, tap an occasion, read results. Everything after that is optional refinement.

3Now — the fussy case

Occasion, drag distance, tap a neighbourhood. Two of those are drags, not taps.

What I'd still argue with

Six is a guess. It fits a phone and it beats a list, but nothing proves six is right — four might read faster, eight might cover more. That's a test, not a decision.

Time-of-day is an assumption about behaviour. Someone booking Saturday dinner on Tuesday morning gets a brunch set and is badly served. There needs to be a way to say "not now, later" without it becoming another filter.

Occasions are culturally loaded. "End of month" is a South African rhythm. "Happy hour" barely exists in Paris. If the tiles are generated per city, the vocabulary has to be too — otherwise the app reads as translated rather than local.

The exit is the weakest stage. Everything above optimises finding; almost none of it optimises the moment someone turns up and it's full. That's where trust is actually won or lost.
